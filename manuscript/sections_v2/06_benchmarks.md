# Results II: how often does it matter?

## Benchmark data: usually fine, occasionally not

Repeated rows are everywhere in the benchmarks. Of the {{n_total}} data sets, {{n_any_dup}} contain at least one repeated feature vector, and in {{n_dup_ge30}} at least 30% of rows belong to a group of identical rows. The exposure formula (2) predicted the share of seen test rows almost exactly (mean absolute error {{exp_mae}}, correlation {{exp_r}} with the share observed over 10 random splits), so this part of the check can be trusted. Per-data-set statistics are in the Supplementary Material (Table S1).

The effect on coverage, however, is usually small. We had planned to test whether the *typical* data set with repeats shows a shortfall on new cases, and it does not. The median gap from 90% across data sets was between {{h1_gap_median_min}} and {{h1_gap_median_max}} percentage points, depending on the learner and score, and none of these one-sided tests was significant after correction for multiple testing (smallest adjusted $p$ = {{h1_min_p_holm}}). For most benchmark data, standard conformal prediction is adequate for new cases too.

The shortfalls are concentrated where the check says they should be. Where fewer than 10% of test rows are seen, no learner fell more than 2 pp short. Where at least 30% are seen, {{x_hi_share2}}% of data set–learner combinations fell more than 2 pp short and {{x_hi_share5}}% more than 5 pp. The worst case was {{h1_worst_gap_cls}} pp in classification and {{h1_worst_gap_reg}} pp in regression. These shortfalls are invisible in the usual overall figure, which stayed at 90% throughout.

Exposure alone does not explain which data sets fail; label purity does much of the rest. Two data sets with the same high exposure show the contrast. In *solar_flare* (about 85% seen test rows) new cases were covered only {{sf_novel_range_pct}} of the time, depending on the learner. In *cps88wages*, with the same exposure, identical worker profiles almost always have different wages, so there is nothing to memorize, and new cases were covered at the nominal rate ({{cps_novel_range_pct}}). Across the {{rp_rf_n}} data sets with repeats, the predicted shortfall $g^*$ of Eq. (3) ranked the observed random-forest shortfalls well (Spearman $\rho$ = {{rp_rf_rho}}, against {{rp_rf_rho_pi}} for exposure alone). It also worked as a floor: in {{rp_rf_above}}% of data sets the observed gap was no worse than $g^*$ minus 2 pp, and the typical random forest reached {{rp_rf_ratio}} of the predicted worst case. Less flexible learners fell short by less, as expected: LightGBM ({{rp_lgbm_rho}}) and the neural network ({{rp_mlp_rho}}) were ranked reasonably, while for logistic regression exposure alone was the better guide ({{rp_logreg_rho_pi}} versus {{rp_logreg_rho}}). We note that this analysis was done on data we had already seen when deriving Eq. (3). The fair test is in Section 7.

The one clear exception is *solar_flare* itself, where the random forest fell about 16 pp short although $g^*$ is only about −4 pp. Its repeated rows disagree on the exact target value, so they do not count as "pure", but the targets differ so little that the model still predicts them almost perfectly. This is precisely the failure of the equal-difficulty assumption mentioned in Section 3. In regression, a practitioner should therefore read the exposure $\bar\pi$, not only $g^*$, as a warning.

## Adding copies on purpose

The benchmark comparison is observational, so we also created the problem deliberately. Appending exact copies to the duplicate-free data sets raised exposure as Eq. (2) predicts, and the shortfall grew with it (Fig. 2). For random forests in classification, the median gap went from {{inj_classification_rf_0_gap}} pp without copies to {{inj_classification_rf_0p5_gap}} pp when half of the rows were copied and {{inj_classification_rf_1_gap}} pp when all were (regression: {{inj_regression_rf_1_gap}} pp). LightGBM behaved similarly ({{inj_classification_lgbm_1_gap}} pp). Regularized linear models, which do not memorize individual rows, were barely affected ({{inj_classification_logreg_1_gap}} pp). In these experiments the predicted shortfall tracked the random-forest gap closely (Spearman $\rho$ = {{rpi_rf_rho}}; median ratio of observed to predicted {{rpi_rf_ratio}} at the highest rate). Separate calibration stayed at the target at every rate ({{inj_classification_rf_1_mcp}} pp for random forests with all rows copied).

We also tried to show the other half of the mechanism by making models less flexible (larger leaves, stronger regularization) on the benchmark data. Coverage of seen rows did drop, as expected, but the change in the new-case gap was too small to detect at these data sets' moderate exposure (trend tests $p$ = {{cap_classification_rf_p}} and {{cap_regression_rf_p}} for random forests). We therefore rely on the learner comparison in the injection experiment, not on this experiment, for the role of model flexibility.

## What the fix costs

Separate calibration restored coverage on new cases across the board. On the benchmark data with repeats, its median gap ranged from {{h2_mcp_median_range}} pp, and the confidence interval of the median stayed above −1 pp in {{h2_noninf_all}} learner–score combinations. Its worst single data set was again solar_flare ({{x_worst_mcp}} pp, against {{x_worst}} pp for the standard method). That data set leaves only about 36 new calibration rows per split, and in 200 extra splits separate calibration averaged 90.9% (95% CI 89.9–91.8%) for LightGBM while the standard method averaged 78%.

This is where the safety margin of Section 3.4 helps: it trades a slightly larger set for protection against an unlucky calibration draw. Its effect on the fresh data is reported in Section 7.

The price in set size is modest. For new cases, sets or intervals grew by a median factor of {{h3_novel_ratio_range}}, while seen cases received tighter sets, so the average over all cases changed by a factor of only {{h3_all_ratio_range}}. Computationally the fix is free: on 30,000-row data, hashing the rows took at most {{cost_keys_max}} s, and computing the two thresholds took {{cost_mcp_us}} µs against {{cost_scp_us}} µs for one threshold. On data without repeats the fix reduces to the standard method exactly (largest difference {{null_maxdiff}}).

Two alternatives are worth knowing. In regression, scaling residuals by the distance to the nearest training rows removed most of the shortfall on its own (median gap {{nb_rf_scp_med}} pp for random forests), though without a guarantee for new cases and with a remaining worst case of {{nb_rf_scp_min}} pp. Splitting the data so that identical rows always stay together (a "group split") also gives honest coverage for new cases (median {{h6_median_range_noknn_pct}}). It changes the training data, though, and it answers only the question "how good is the model on new cases", whereas separate calibration keeps the usual split and covers any mix of seen and new cases.
