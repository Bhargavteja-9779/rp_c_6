# Results III: fresh data and near-copies

## A fair test on data we had not seen

The predicted-gap formula (3) was derived after we had seen the benchmark results, so the analyses above could flatter it. We therefore fixed the analysis plan in a time-stamped commit and then selected new data sets from two collections we had not used: the AutoML Benchmark (Gijsbers et al. 2024) and the tabular benchmark of Grinsztajn et al. (2022). The selection used no labels and no models. It kept data sets in which at least 1% of test rows are expected to be seen, and it dropped data sets we had already used, data sets that appear twice in these collections, and five whose download failed. This left {{pr_n}} data sets ({{pr_n_cls}} classification, {{pr_n_reg}} regression), each analysed with 20 random splits. The full selection log, with the reason for every exclusion, and per-data-set results are in the Supplementary Material (Tables R2–R7). The plan named five tests in advance. Two were clearly supported and three were not, and we report all five.

*The fix works on new data.* Separate calibration kept the coverage of new cases at the target for all three learners. The median gap was {{pr3_rf_MCP_med}} pp for random forests (worst data set {{pr3_rf_MCP_worst}} pp), {{pr3_lgbm_MCP_med}} pp for LightGBM (worst {{pr3_lgbm_MCP_worst}} pp) and {{pr3_logreg_MCP_med}} pp for the linear models (worst {{pr3_logreg_MCP_worst}} pp). Without the fix, the worst data set's gap was {{pr3_rf_SCP_worst}} pp (random forest) and {{pr3_lgbm_SCP_worst}} pp (LightGBM). The fix made new-case sets larger by a median factor of only {{pr_size_mcp}}, because on most of these data sets there was little to fix. In one data set (seattlecrime6) almost every test row is seen, and the fix returned the full set for new cases in {{pr_fb_rate}}% of splits, because too few new calibration rows existed to do anything else.

*The safety margin reduces unlucky splits.* With separate calibration, coverage of new cases fell below 87% in {{pr4_rf_mcp}}% of individual splits (random forest). With the safety margin this dropped to {{pr4_rf_pac}}%, and similarly for the other learners ({{pr4_lgbm_mcp}}% → {{pr4_lgbm_pac}}% and {{pr4_logreg_mcp}}% → {{pr4_logreg_pac}}%). The cost was sets about {{pr_size_pac}} times as large. Because most data sets had no unlucky splits at all, this planned test did not reach significance after correction for multiple testing (adjusted $p$ = {{pr_holm_P4}}).

*The predicted gap works as a limit, not as a ranking.* In all {{pr_n}} data sets, the random forest's actual gap stayed within 2 pp of the predicted worst case or above it ({{pr_rf_above}}%; 95% CI {{pr_rf_above_ci}}%), as planned. LightGBM behaved the same, and the linear models had one exception, discussed below. However, the predicted gap did **not** rank the fresh data sets by how badly they fell short: the planned rank correlation for random forests was weak and not significant ($\rho$ = {{pr_rf_rho}}, $p$ = {{pr_holm_P1}}), and the advantage over exposure alone was not established (difference in $\rho$, 95% CI {{pr_rf_diffci}}). For LightGBM the correlation was {{pr_lgbm_rho}} ($p$ = {{pr_lgbm_p}}, not corrected). Two things explain this. Most fresh data sets have essentially no shortfall, so their ranks are decided by noise. And on some of the most exposed classification sets the random forest was *conservative* on new cases instead of falling short. On airlines, for example, the predicted worst case was {{pr_air_gs}} pp but the observed gap was {{pr_air_rf_gap}} pp, most likely because many identical calibration rows produce tied scores (Section 5).

*The check tells safe from unsafe data.* For a practitioner, ranking matters less than the decision "fix or not". Here the check did well (Fig. 4). Before these results existed, our draft guidance said that a data set with a predicted gap no worse than −2 pp and fewer than 10% seen test rows needs no fix (Section 8.1). Of the fresh data sets, {{pr_rule_n}} met this rule, and the worst gap among them, over all three learners, was {{pr_rule_worst}} pp. In the other direction, label purity removed false alarms that exposure alone would raise. In black_friday ({{pr_bf_pi}} seen test rows), seattlecrime6 ({{pr_sc_pi}}) and superconduct ({{pr_cond_pi}}), repeated rows almost never agree on the exact target, so purity predicts no shortfall even though exposure alone predicts {{pr_bf_gp}}, {{pr_sc_gp}} and {{pr_cond_gp}} pp. The random forest's actual gaps were {{pr_bf_rf_gap}}, {{pr_sc_rf_gap}} and {{pr_cond_rf_gap}} pp. Of the {{pr_gp_nflag}} data sets that exposure alone flags, the purity-based check flags {{pr_gs_nflag}}, and among these are all data sets where the random forest or LightGBM fell more than 2 pp short ({{pr_gs_rf_hit}} and {{pr_gs_lgbm_hit}} data sets). The clearest case is analcatdata_supreme, where {{pr_sup_pip}} of test rows have a training copy with exactly the same target. The check predicted a worst case of {{pr_sup_gs}} pp, and the observed gaps on new cases were {{pr_sup_rf_gap}} pp (random forest) and {{pr_sup_lgbm_gap}} pp (LightGBM).

The one miss was a linear model on black_friday, whose gap was {{pr_bf_logreg_gap}} pp although the purity-based check predicted no problem. A linear model cannot memorize rows, so this shortfall is not the memorization effect the check is built for; exposure alone did flag this data set. This again suggests reading both numbers, as recommended in Section 8.1.

## When copies are not exact

Real duplicates are often not byte-identical: a value is rounded differently, or a record is re-measured. To see what this does, we appended copies of half of the rows to each of the same {{nd_n}} duplicate-free data sets and added a little noise to the copies' numeric features, from none up to 10% of each feature's standard deviation. Because we created the copies ourselves, we know which test rows are *truly* new, meaning that no version of the same record is in the training data.

Even very small noise blinds the exact-match check: at every noise level above zero it recognized none of the copies ({{nd_detect_cla}}%; Fig. 5). The problem itself does not go away, though. With exact copies, the random forest's coverage gap on truly new cases was {{nd_cla_0p0_SCP}} pp in classification. With noise of 0.1%, 1% and 10% of a standard deviation, it was {{nd_cla_0p001_SCP}}, {{nd_cla_0p01_SCP}} and {{nd_cla_0p1_SCP}} pp (regression: {{nd_reg_0p0_SCP}}, {{nd_reg_0p001_SCP}}, {{nd_reg_0p01_SCP}} and {{nd_reg_0p1_SCP}} pp). Small amounts of noise leave most of the shortfall in place; only noise of 10% of a standard deviation removes most of it in regression. The exact-match fix cannot see these copies: once noise is added, its result is close to that of the standard method ({{nd_cla_0p001_MCP_exact}} pp at the smallest noise level in classification).

Calibrating by *distance* to the nearest training rows does handle this case. We split calibration and test rows into six groups by that distance (exact copies form one group) and calibrated each group separately. Coverage of truly new cases then stayed at the target at every noise level ({{nd_cla_0p0_DCP}}, {{nd_cla_0p001_DCP}}, {{nd_cla_0p01_DCP}} and {{nd_cla_0p1_DCP}} pp in classification; {{nd_reg_0p0_DCP}}, {{nd_reg_0p001_DCP}}, {{nd_reg_0p01_DCP}} and {{nd_reg_0p1_DCP}} pp in regression), as good as an idealized version that knows the true record identity ({{nd_cla_0p01_MCP_oracle}} pp at 1% noise). In practice, then, the exact check is the right first step because it is simple and explains *why* coverage fails, and distance-based calibration is the safer fix whenever near-copies are plausible.
